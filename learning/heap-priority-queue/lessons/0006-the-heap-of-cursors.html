The Heap of Cursors: Merging Sorted Sources

Lesson 6 · ~30 min (lesson 12 min + drill 18 min) · ← Lesson 5 · cheat sheet

This lesson teaches exactly one new skill: a heap whose entries are positions into data owned by other structures. Until now, every heap you built owned its items. This one owns nothing — it is a set of bookmarks. As always, the problem comes first.

1. The problem

Your system runs several services. Each service writes its own log: a list of records, each with a tick t and a message, and each list is already in time order — appends come from a clock that only moves forward, the same reason the queue in your last problem never needed sorting. The logs live in a dict:

services = {
    "api": [{"t": 1, "msg": "api-1"}, {"t": 4, "msg": "api-2"}],
    "db":  [{"t": 1, "msg": "db-1"},  {"t": 9, "msg": "db-2"}],
    "web": [{"t": 2, "msg": "web-1"}],
}

Your job (the drill's Part B): produce the one merged timeline, smaller t first; on a t tie across services the smaller service name goes first; within one service, original order is preserved. Worked in full on the input above:

t=1  api-1     (tie with db-1 at t=1 → "api" < "db")
t=1  db-1
t=2  web-1
t=4  api-2
t=9  db-2

Two facts about real deployments make this interesting: N, the total record count, is huge — and the caller very often wants only the first page: the first r records, with r tiny. Hold both; they decide everything below.

2. Two ways that work — and what each one wastes

Dump and re-sort. Concatenate all N records, sort the pile. Correct — and it throws away the one thing you were given for free: every piece was already sorted. You pay O(N log N) as if the input were random noise, and you cannot stop early: the sort finishes before the first record is usable.

Scan the heads. Better instinct: the next record of the merged timeline must be the earliest unconsumed record of some service — one of k candidates, the "head" of each list. So: compare the k heads, take the smallest, advance that list, repeat. Correct — and each of the N steps rescans all k heads: O(N·k).

Read the second one again. A set of candidates that changes at every step, and the same question asked each time: "best available right now?" — you met this exact sentence in Lesson 5. That question, asked repeatedly against a moving set, is the heap's one job. The scan is a correct algorithm paying the heap's price in the wrong coin.

3. The fix: a heap of cursors

Put the k heads in a heap — not the records, positions: tuples (t, service, position). The data never moves; it stays in the dict's lists.

The invariant The heap holds exactly one entry per unfinished source — its head. It never owns the data; it holds bookmarks into data owned elsewhere. Its size is k, whatever N is. 
seed — one bookmark per non-empty source, heapify
while the heap is not empty:
    pop the smallest (t, service, position)   — that record is next
    output it (read straight from the dict)
    advance — if that service has a record at position + 1, push it

Pop the winner, push the winner's successor from the same list. Here is the full trace on the section 1 input. Notation: api₀¹ means "bookmark into api at position 0, whose record has t = 1". This table is the lesson — if you can reproduce it on paper for a different input, you have the pattern:

heap (the frontier)moveoutput so far
api₀¹ · db₀¹ · web₀²seed all three heads[]
db₀¹ · web₀² · api₁⁴pop api₀ (t-tie vs db₀ → name), push api₁[api-1]
web₀² · api₁⁴ · db₁⁹pop db₀, push db₁[api-1, db-1]
api₁⁴ · db₁⁹pop web₀ — web is exhausted, no push[api-1, db-1, web-1]
db₁⁹pop api₁ — api is exhausted, no push[api-1, db-1, web-1, api-2]
—pop db₁ — heap empty, done[api-1, db-1, web-1, api-2, db-2]

Every row, the heap holds at most three bookmarks — one per service still alive. It never grows with N. And nothing here is min-only: newest-first instead of oldest-first is the same machinery with Lesson 4's direction decision flipped. Nothing else changes.

Mid-merge, what does the heap contain?

One head per unfinished source — the frontier. Push everything and you have rebuilt dump-and-re-sort with extra steps, at heap size N. Exhausted sources have nothing left to offer and simply drop out of the heap.

4. The tuple, one more time

You have designed heap tuples twice now — key and direction in Lesson 4, the three-level tie rule in Lesson 5. This one adds a twist: the records are dicts, and dicts cannot be ordered. If a comparison ever reaches one, the merge dies with a TypeError. Each field of (t, service, position) is doing a job:

  • t — the merge order itself;
  • service — the spec's tiebreak: smaller name first on a t tie;
  • position — preserves within-service order, and makes the prefix unique: no two entries can tie on all three fields, so a comparison always stops before any payload could be reached.

Tuple comparison stops at the first field that differs — a unique comparable prefix is a fence the comparison can never climb over. This is not a trick of this lesson; it is the documented pattern for real-world priority queues, straight from the heapq priority-queue implementation notes: store (priority, count, task) where count is a unique sequence number, precisely so task — arbitrary, unorderable — is never compared.

The payload rides behind the fence Whether the payload sits in the tuple after a unique prefix or stays out of the heap entirely (looked up by (service, position), as here), the rule is the same: everything that decides order comes first, is comparable, and cannot fully tie. The drill has a test whose only purpose is to raise TypeError the moment your entries can reach a record.

What makes (t, service, position) a safe heap entry?

Uniqueness of the prefix. Tuple comparison walks fields left to right until one differs; (service, position) is unique per record, so a decision is always reached. It does not stop at the first field — it stops at the first difference. Dicts do not order at all, and heapq has no idea what a record is.

5. The cost, per phase — and what laziness buys

Notation as in the drill: k sources, N total records, r records requested. Full merge, phases first:

PhaseWhat runsCost
Seedheapify one head per sourceO(k)
Loopeach record enters the heap once, leaves once; heap never exceeds kO(N log k)
Outputbuild the merged listO(N)

Dominant: the loop, O(N log k) — against the baseline's O(N log N). The gap is log k vs log N: the price of a heap operation is the log of the heap's size, and the frontier keeps that size at k, not N. Same accounting as Lesson 5: per record, not per iteration. (The loop's bound itself you already own: Q = records not yet output, down by one every iteration — no pairing argument even needed here.)

Now the first page. The loop above has a property dump-and-re-sort can never have: after r pops, the first r records of the answer are complete and correct — so stop. Seed the heads, pop r times, each pop pushing at most one successor:

first r records: O(k + r log k) — the other N − r records are never touched.

With N in the millions and r = 10, that is the difference between a query and a batch job. The heap did not get faster; it got lazier — it does exactly as much of the merge as is consumed, because at every moment it holds the complete answer to "what comes next?".

You need only the first r records of the merge. What do you save?

Everything past the r-th record is never popped, never pushed, never read: O(k + r log k). The seed is O(k), not O(N) — heads only. The heap's size was never N to begin with (the frontier), and there is no sort anywhere in this algorithm — that is the point of it.

6. The standard library knows this loop

What you are about to build by hand exists as heapq.merge(*iterables, key=None, reverse=False) — it takes already-sorted inputs, returns a lazy iterator over the merged whole, and its implementation is a heap of cursors. So why build it yourself? Because in problems — and in interviews — the merge is rarely the whole task: you will need your own direction, your own tie rule, your own stopping point, and the tuple that encodes them is exactly what heapq.merge hides. Per the house rule, don't take my word for the mechanism: after the drill, read its source — you will recognize every move, cursor advance and all.

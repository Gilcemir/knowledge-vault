Two Heaps, One Boundary

Lesson 7 · ~30 min (lesson 12 min + drill 18 min) · ← Lesson 6 · cheat sheet

This lesson teaches exactly one new skill: maintaining an ordered boundary through a changing population, with two heaps facing each other. Every heap so far served one end of one group. This time the interesting spot is the middle — the line where one group ends and the other begins. As always, the problem comes first.

1. The problem

Your CI cluster has s build machines (slots). Jobs arrive one at a time, each with a priority. The rule the whole team agreed on: at every moment, the s strongest jobs are the ones running — everyone else waits. "Stronger" means higher priority; on a priority tie, the job submitted earlier outranks. If a new job outranks the weakest running one, that running job is preempted: kicked back to the waiting pool, mid-build, no mercy.

Two questions must be answerable instantly, because the dashboard polls them constantly:

  • weakest running — the job on the chopping block, the one a newcomer would preempt;
  • strongest waiting — the job next in line, the one a freed machine would take.

And the boundary itself moves: a machine comes online (add_slot — the strongest waiting job is promoted) or dies (remove_slot — the weakest running job is demoted). A worked session, s = 2:

submit d(4)          → runs (farm not full)
submit e(9)          → runs (farm not full)
submit f(6)          → f outranks d(4): d is preempted → f runs, d waits
weakest_running?     → f          strongest_waiting?  → d
submit g(8)          → g outranks f(6): f is preempted → g runs, f waits
weakest_running?     → g          strongest_waiting?  → f

Notation before any costs, so no letters collide: n = jobs currently in the farm (both groups together), s = slots. n grows without bound; s is small and moves by one.

2. Two designs that work — and what each one wastes

One list, kept sorted. Keep every job in one list ordered by strength; the boundary is just index s. Both queries are O(1) reads — lovely. But every submit must insert into the middle of a sorted list, and insertion shifts everything after the slot: O(n) per submit, even using bisect.insort — the docs say it plainly: the search is O(log n), "the slow O(n) insertion step" dominates. A million submits → a trillion shifts. The order you maintain is total: every job perfectly ranked against every other. The dashboard only ever asks about two of them.

One heap. A single max-heap of all jobs answers "strongest overall" in O(1) — but the questions live at the boundary, not the top. "Weakest running" is a min-question asked of the top group, and Lesson 4 was blunt about this: a heap serves one end. Getting the other end out of it is an O(n) scan. Preempting — removing something from the middle of a heap — is no better.

Look at what the two failures have in common. The sorted list maintains too much order; the single heap maintains order in the wrong place. Everything the dashboard asks — who gets preempted, who gets promoted — happens at exactly one line: the cut between running and waiting.

3. The fix: cut the population, point both roots at the cut

Split the jobs into the two groups they already form. Give each group its own heap, and choose each direction so that the root is the group's boundary-side element:

  • running → a min-heap: its root is the weakest runner;
  • waiting → a max-heap: its root is the strongest waiter.

The two roots face each other across the cut like sentries. Both dashboard questions are now root peeks — O(1). And every event in the farm's life is a move at the roots:

submit(job):
    if the farm is not full:            push job onto running
    elif job outranks running's root:   pop that root, push it onto waiting,
                                        push job onto running          (preempt)
    else:                               push job onto waiting

add_slot:     if anyone waits:  pop waiting's root  → push onto running (promote)
remove_slot:  if over capacity: pop running's root  → push onto waiting (demote)
The invariant Every running job outranks every waiting job. Each operation above preserves it, so it holds forever — and it is what makes the two roots meaningful: running's min and waiting's max are not just each group's edge, they are the two jobs adjacent to the boundary in the whole population's order.

Full trace of a session, s starting at 2. Heaps are written root-first — g8 means job g, priority 8. Reproduce this table on paper for a different input and you have the pattern:

moverunning (min-heap)waiting (max-heap)weakest run · strongest wait

Watch g8 in the last two rows: demoted and promoted, it crossed the boundary twice — and both times it was a root when it moved. That is not luck; it is the design. The job adjacent to the cut is, by the invariant, always sitting at a root, already in O(1) reach.

The farm holds 10,000 jobs. How many jobs can a single submit move across the boundary?

A submit changes the population by one, so the boundary shifts by at most one position — and the job sitting at that position is a root by the invariant. One pop, one push, done. Nothing deeper in either heap is disturbed, which is the entire reason the operation is O(log n) and not O(n).

4. The tuples: two mirrored encodings of one order

There is one strength order — priority first, then earlier submission — but Python's heapq only builds min-heaps, so each side encodes that order in its own direction (Lesson 4's negation trick, now applied twice, in opposite orientations):

arrival = next(counter)                # unique, ever-increasing — the tie-breaker

running entry:  ( priority, -arrival, name)   # min-root = weakest  (lowest p; tie → latest)
waiting entry:  (-priority,  arrival, name)   # min-root = strongest (highest p; tie → earliest)

Read the tie logic twice — it points opposite ways on purpose. Among equal priorities, the weakest is the one submitted last (earlier outranks), hence -arrival on the running side; the strongest waiter is the one submitted first, hence bare arrival on the waiting side. The unique arrival also settles every comparison before name is ever reached — Lesson 6's comparable-prefix fence, still on duty.

Re-encode at the border A job crossing the boundary cannot keep its tuple. Popped from running as (p, -a, name), it must be pushed to waiting as (-p, a, name) — every sign flips. Push the old tuple unchanged and the destination heap orders it nonsensically; nothing crashes, the answers just go quietly wrong. The drill has a test that exists only to catch this.
A peek is not a pop Both queries read heap[0] and touch nothing. A query that pops "just to look" — or aliases and mutates shared state, as a certain Lesson 6 code review found — changes the farm as a side effect of observing it. The drill runs your queries repeatedly and checks the farm behaves as if they had never happened.

Which direction does each side's heap point?

Point each root at the boundary: the running group's boundary-side element is its weakest (min-heap), the waiting group's is its strongest (max-heap). Lesson 4's rule — direction opposite to what you keep, or here: direction toward what you must see — decides both in one breath.

5. What it costs

Every operation is a constant number of root moves, and a root move on a heap of at most n entries is O(log n):

operationheap movescost
submit — farm not full1 pushO(log n)
submit — preemption1 pop + 2 pushesO(log n)
submit — straight to waiting1 pushO(log n)
weakest running / strongest waiting0 — peek heap[0]O(1)
add_slot / remove_slot≤ 1 pop + 1 pushO(log n)
n submits, totalO(n log n)

Against the baselines: the sorted list pays O(n) per submit — O(n²) total — to maintain a total order nobody asked for; the single heap pays O(n) per query to dig out the end it doesn't serve. The two-heap design maintains exactly as much order as the questions require: perfect knowledge at the cut, cheerful ignorance everywhere else. That economy — order only where the questions are — is the pattern's whole identity.

One submit into a farm of n jobs costs…

Worst case is the preemption path: pop one root, push twice — three O(log n) moves, constant count. Nothing rescans (the roots already face the boundary), and nothing amortizes to O(1) (each move genuinely sifts a log-depth path, every time).

6. Sources

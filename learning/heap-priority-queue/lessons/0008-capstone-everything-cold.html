Rules of the capstone First pass closed-book — no cheat sheet, no lesson tabs, no peeking at old code. A wrong answer is not a failure; it is a measurement telling you which page of the cheat sheet earns a re-read. After the full pass, review freely.

1. The quiz — six questions, shuffled across seven lessons

You need the k LARGEST items from a stream, memory-bounded. Which heap does the job?

Direction opposite to the selection: keeping the largest means the root must expose the weakest survivor — the one a newcomer has to beat, and the one that gets evicted. A max root would show you the champion, who is never the one in danger. Size k, never n: the heap holds survivors, not history. (Lesson 4.)

A max-heap via negation stores (-priority, ???). Earlier arrival must win priority ties. What goes in?

Negate only what must be reversed. The heap is physically a min-heap, so "wins" = "compares smaller". Highest priority must win → negate it. Earliest arrival must win, and earlier is already smaller → leave it alone. The tiebreaker's sign follows what should win in the heap's real direction, field by field — never "negate everything because it's a max-heap". (Lessons 4–5, and a bug you fixed twice.)

A loop alternates clock-jumps (Q frozen) with executions (Q shrinks). Why is the total still O(Q)?

The pairing argument: every jump lands exactly where something becomes eligible, so the very next iteration executes and shrinks Q. Charge each frozen iteration to its executing neighbor: #jumps ≤ #executions ≤ Q, total ≤ 2Q, constant vanishes. You produced this reasoning unprompted on Task Scheduler. (Lesson 5 / LC 621.)

Mid-merge of k sorted lists holding N records in total, the heap's size stays…

One head per unfinished source — exhausted sources drop out, so the frontier shrinks below k near the end (hence "never above", not "always exactly"). It is never anywhere near N: the heap holds bookmarks, not data, and log(heap size) = log k is the entire reason the merge beats re-sorting. (Lesson 6.)

The exchange argument proves a greedy choice safe by showing what?

Take any competing solution, swap one choice to agree with greedy, show the result is no worse — repeat until it IS the greedy solution, never having lost ground. That is what licenses "take the best now and never look back". The heap is just the machine that finds "best now" in O(log n); the argument is what makes using it correct. (Lesson 5, Erickson Ch. 4.)

What does the two-heaps pattern actually maintain, and where does it maintain it?

Perfect knowledge at the boundary, cheerful ignorance everywhere else. Neither heap is sorted internally, no global order exists — and that economy is exactly why every operation is O(log n) while the sorted-list baseline pays O(n) per insert maintaining an order nobody queries. (Lesson 7.)

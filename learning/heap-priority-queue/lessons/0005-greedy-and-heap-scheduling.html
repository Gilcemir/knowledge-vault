Greedy and the Heap: Scheduling Under a Gate

Lesson 5 · ~30 min (lesson 12 min + drill 18 min) · ← Lesson 4 · cheat sheet

This lesson teaches exactly one new skill: running a heap when some items are not allowed yet. Every idea in it — the pattern, the loop, the cost argument, even the name "greedy" — exists to solve one concrete problem. So the problem comes first.

1. The problem

A shop receives orders during the day. Each order is a tuple (arrival, priority, name):

  • arrival — the tick at which the order arrives. Before this tick, the order does not exist as far as the worker is concerned.
  • priority — how important it is. Bigger number = more important.
  • name — just an identifier.

A single worker handles one order per tick, and every order takes exactly one tick. The rule the worker follows, every time they are free:

The worker's rule Among the orders that have already arrived and are not yet handled, take the one with the highest priority. If nothing has arrived yet, wait.

Your job (this is the drill's Part B): given the list of orders, return the names in the order the worker handles them.

One small input, worked in full. Take these three orders — note they are not given in arrival order:

orders = [(2, 4, "x"), (0, 1, "y"), (3, 8, "z")]
tick 0: arrived so far: only y (arrival 0).      → handle y
tick 1: x arrives at 2, z at 3 — nothing yet.    → wait
tick 2: x has arrived.                            → handle x
tick 3: z has arrived.                            → handle z

answer: ["y", "x", "z"]

Notice what happened at tick 0: z has priority 8, by far the best in the input — and the worker handled y, priority 1. Not because the rule is subtle, but because z did not exist yet. That is the whole novelty of this lesson: an item can be temporarily ineligible. It may well be the best thing in the input, and you still cannot take it yet.

2. Why the Lesson 4 reflex fails here

Your reflex after four lessons is: highest priority first → max-heap → push everything keyed by -priority, pop repeatedly. Try it on the example:

push all three:  heap root = z   (priority 8, the maximum)
pop → z ???      but it is tick 0 and z only arrives at tick 3

The root of the heap is an order from the future. And a heap gives you no way to ask for the second-best without removing the first — peeking past an ineligible root is not an operation you have. So "the answer is at h[0]" breaks, for the first time in this track. The fix is structural, and it is small.

3. The fix: two containers and a clock

Keep ineligible items out of the heap entirely. They wait somewhere else, and they enter the heap only at the moment they become eligible.

The invariant The heap contains only items eligible right now. Everything else waits in a second container, tagged with the time it becomes eligible. Every bug in this pattern is a violation of that one sentence.

Concretely: a waiting area (the orders, sorted by arrival — a list plus an index, or a deque) and the heap (only arrived orders, keyed by your greedy criterion). A clock variable now. The loop has three moves:

while work remains:
    release  — move everything with arrival <= now from waiting into the heap
    if the heap is empty:
        jump the clock to the next arrival time
        continue
    act      — pop the best, record it, now += 1

Here is the full trace on the example. This table is the lesson — if you can reproduce it on paper for a different input, you have the pattern:

nowwaiting (by arrival)heap (eligible only)moveoutput so far
0y(0) · x(2) · z(3)—release y[]
0x(2) · z(3)y¹act: pop y, now→1[y]
1x(2) · z(3)—heap empty → jump now→2[y]
2z(3)x⁴release x; act: pop x, now→3[y, x]
3—z⁸release z; act: pop z, now→4[y, x, z]

(Superscripts are priorities.) Two details worth pausing on:

  • Release must be cheap. It is not a filter over the whole input each time. Because the waiting area is sorted by arrival, you walk it from the front and stop at the first order that has not arrived — each order is looked at once, ever. That is why sorting up front is not optional.
  • The key and direction decisions from Lesson 4 come back unchanged. Highest priority first → max-heap → negate. Ties inside the heap are settled by what you put in the tuple, exactly as in Lesson 4.

The gate here is one-way: once an order has arrived, it stays arrived. A gate can also repeat — an item becomes ineligible again each time you use it. Same two containers, same invariant; the only change is that act parks the item back in the waiting area instead of discarding it. That variation is deliberately left for you to build on the next problem.

In the two-container pattern, what may the heap contain?

Eligible items only — that is the whole point of the second container. Keeping ineligible items in the heap and marking them stale is a real technique for a different problem (deleting from a heap you cannot search), but here it puts an untouchable item at the root and you cannot see past it.

4. How many times does that loop run?

Look at the loop again. There is no for i in range(n) to read the answer off — the iteration count depends on the data. To bound it you need a small proof technique, and it is the argument you did not reach for on P2, where the loop ran "until fewer than two stones remain".

The recipe: find the shrinking quantity

Find a quantity Q such that:

  1. Q is a non-negative integer — it cannot descend forever;
  2. every iteration decreases it by at least 1;
  3. its starting value is bounded in terms of the input.

Then the loop runs at most Qstart times. That is the entire technique; the work is in picking Q.

Worked on P2: let Q = stones in the heap. Each iteration pops two, pushes at most one — Q drops by 1 or 2, at least 1 either way. Starts at n, stops at Q ≤ 1: at most n − 1 iterations, each O(log n), so O(n log n). You gave that total on P2 without the argument; the argument is what tells you which variable the bound is in — and here that is about to matter.

The trap: an iteration that consumes nothing

Try Q = "orders not yet handled" on the scheduling loop. The act branch decreases it by one — fine. But look at the trace, row now = 1: the heap was empty, the worker waited, and nothing was handled. If the clock advances one tick at a time, that idle row repeats once per tick, Q unchanged, and condition (2) fails. Your bound is not a bound.

Two ways out, and they produce the same output but wildly different costs:

Clock policyDoes every iteration consume an order?Iterations bounded by
Advance one tick at a timeNo — idle ticks consume nothingT, the last arrival time
Jump straight to the next arrivalYes — an idle jump is immediately followed by an actn, the number of orders

T came with the data; it has no relationship to n. Five orders whose arrivals run to 109 mean a tick-by-tick loop does 109 iterations of nothing. Nobody notices this reading the code — you notice it by trying to name Q and failing. The drill has a test built exactly on this.

When no single Q works Sometimes an iteration decreases one quantity and leaves another alone, and vice versa. Then bound the sum: if every iteration decreases Q1 + Q2 by at least 1, the sum is your Q. "Orders waiting + orders unarrived" is the usual shape.

Why does jumping the clock to the next eligibility time matter?

It is what makes every iteration consume something, so the item count becomes a valid Q. The heap holds the same items either way, ties are settled by the key you chose, and correctness of the choice is section 6's business — a clock policy cannot fix a wrong choice.

5. The cost, per phase

Same table format as always — phases first, dominance second, aggregate last. For n orders through the loop, with the clock-jumping policy:

PhaseWhat runsCost
Preparesort the orders by arrival timeO(n log n)
Loop≤ n iterations; each order pushed once, popped onceO(n log n)
Outputbuild the answer listO(n)

Two phases tie at O(n log n), and that is worth seeing once: "which phase dominates" does not always have exactly one winner. What it never has is zero winners — the aggregate is O(n log n), and it comes from naming the phases, not from recognising the shape.

The push-once-pop-once accounting is the part to keep. You are not paying log n per iteration; you are paying it per order, twice, across the whole run. That is why the release step must never rescan.

6. What you just did has a name: greedy

The worker's rule — take the best available option, never reconsider a past choice — is a greedy algorithm. No backtracking, no table of subproblems. The heap's role in any greedy algorithm is always the same one it played here: the option set keeps changing (things arrive, get consumed), and the heap answers "best available right now" in O(log n) against a moving set. If the options never changed, you would sort once and walk the list.

"Take the best now" is a strong claim: you are asserting that a decision made with partial information will still look right at the end. Sometimes it is false. The smallest example, in full:

Making change. You have unlimited coins of denominations 1, 3, and 4, and you must pay exactly 6 using as few coins as possible. Try the greedy rule "take the biggest coin that does not exceed what you still owe":

owe 6 → biggest coin ≤ 6 is 4 → take 4, owe 2
owe 2 → biggest coin ≤ 2 is 1 → take 1, owe 1   (3 does not fit in 2)
owe 1 → take 1, owe 0

greedy:   4 + 1 + 1  → 3 coins
optimal:  3 + 3      → 2 coins

Grabbing the 4 felt like progress and it ruined the answer, because it left a remainder only 1s can pay. So a greedy choice needs a reason it cannot cost you later. The standard reason is the exchange argument: assume an optimal solution that disagrees with your choice, swap it to agree with you, and show the swap never makes things worse. Repeat the swap and their optimum becomes yours. Jeff Erickson works this proof in his Algorithms, and names the technique outright:

To show that the greedy algorithm is actually correct, we proved that the output of any other algorithm can be improved by some sort of exchange.
— Erickson, Algorithms, p.160

In an interview you will rarely write the full proof. You will be asked "why is greedy correct here?", and "swapping any two of my choices cannot improve the result, because…" is the shape of the answer. (For the worker's rule it goes through: swapping a lower-priority order into an earlier slot never helps. For the coins, it does not — that is the difference.) If you cannot even sketch the swap, that is your signal the problem may not be greedy at all.

What licenses you to never revisit a greedy choice?

The exchange argument. Tests show absence of counterexamples among cases you thought of; the coin example above passes a lot of tests before it fails. Termination is a separate obligation — section 4 — and a heap is only the machinery for finding the choice, never a reason the choice is right.

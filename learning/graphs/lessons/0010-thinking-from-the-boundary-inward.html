L5 — Thinking From the Boundary Inward

Graphs track · lesson 5 of the main line · ~25 min · 2026-09-17 · prereq: L2 — DFS with Cycles, L4 — BFS: Layers Are Distances · reference: BFS, DFS

The problem first — a question pointing the wrong way

A grid of terrain heights. Rain falls on every cell. Water flows from a cell to any of its four neighbours whose height is less than or equal to its own; it never flows uphill. The question:

Which cells drain to the east edge of the map?

Read that literally and it tells you what to write. For each cell, start a flood there, follow the water, see whether it ever lands on the east edge. You already own that flood — it's your L2 DFS with a condition on the neighbour test. So:

for each of the m·n cells:
    flood from that cell        # O(m·n) in the worst case
    did it touch the east edge?

It is correct. Now price it. On a modest 200×200 grid that is 40 000 floods, each up to 40 000 cells: 1 600 000 000 cell visits. Your P4 solution touched 40 000. The literal reading of the question costs you a factor of forty thousand.

The shape of the trouble The question asks about arrows pointing toward a target. A traversal can only walk arrows away from where it starts. Every flood you run is an attempt to cover that mismatch by brute force.

New: the grid's edges now have a direction

In P1 through P5, adjacency was symmetric: if A was a neighbour of B, then B was a neighbour of A. Walls and Gates, Rotting Oranges, Number of Islands — all of them. Water breaks that.

A directed edge is an edge you may cross in one stated direction only. Here the grid still has four neighbours per cell, but between A and B there is an edge A→B and no edge B→A. The picture above is the whole of the new vocabulary: same cells, same adjacency, one-way arrows.

The move: turn every arrow around

If the trouble is that arrows point the wrong way, reverse them. That object has a name. From CP-Algorithms:

"Let GT denote the transpose graph of G, obtained by reversing the edge directions in G."
— CP-Algorithms, Strongly Connected Components (verified 2026-09-17)

Also called the reverse graph. Now the claim that makes it useful:

The inversion X can reach T in G  ≡  T can reach X in GT.

Not a theorem you need to memorise — it is the definition, read slowly. A path from X to T in G is a list of cells: X, c1, c2, …, T, where every consecutive pair has an arrow. Reverse the arrows and that same list, read right to left — T, …, c2, c1, X — has an arrow at every consecutive pair too. The path did not go anywhere. Only your reading direction changed.

So instead of asking every cell "do you reach the edge?", stand on the edge and ask once: "walking backwards, who do I reach?" One traversal. O(m·n).

The part that bites: what the reversed arrow actually tests

You never build GT as a data structure. You build it by rewriting one line — the neighbour condition. Get this line backwards and the code runs happily and answers a different question.

In G you stand on A and consider neighbour B; the arrow A→B exists when h[B] <= h[A]. In GT that same arrow is traversed the other way: you stand on B and step to A. The condition is unchanged — but you must now express it from B's point of view, with the cell you're standing on first:

Why this is easy to get wrong Nothing about the water changed. The inequality flipped only because the subject of the sentence changed from the higher cell to the lower one. If you reverse a search and leave the condition alone, you have reversed nothing.

Seeding the whole boundary at once

The east edge is not one cell, it's a column of them. You already solved this at P5: put every source in seen and in the queue before the first pop. The formal name for the problem you were solving is worth having —

"Given a digraph and a set of source vertices, is there a directed path from any vertex in the set to v?"
— Sedgewick & Wayne, Algorithms §4.2, Directed Graphs, "multiple-source reachability" (verified 2026-09-17)

— and notice the word digraph. Multi-source seeding was never a grid trick; it is the standard way to ask a reachability question about a whole set at once. Your gates, your rotten oranges and this edge are the same construction.

Check one forward Take (2,0), height 6 — the reversed search says it drains. Walk it forwards, downhill: 6 → (2,1) h=2 → (2,2) h=1, east edge. ✓ Now do (1,0), height 4, which the search says does not drain. Its only downhill neighbour is (0,0) h=2; from there, (0,1) h=1; from there — nothing lower, and (0,2) h=3 is uphill. Dead end. ✓

The algorithm did not get cleverer. You asked the question in the direction the machine can actually walk.

Check yourself

The naive plan runs one flood per cell. What is its total cost?

m·n floods, each visiting up to m·n cells. The visited set makes one flood linear — it does nothing about running m·n of them.

Water flows A→B when h[B] ≤ h[A]. Standing on a cell in the reversed grid, which neighbours do you step to?

The water rule never changed; the subject of the sentence did. Standing on the lower cell and stepping to the higher one, the same edge reads h[nxt] ≥ h[cur] — uphill.

What does seeding every boundary cell into the queue before the first pop buy you?

Multiple-source reachability: a set of sources, one traversal. Exactly what your gates did in P5 and your rotten oranges in P4.

What makes the reversed search give the same answer as the naive one?

It is the definition of GT read slowly — the same list of cells, the same consecutive pairs, read right to left. Option 1 is exactly what stops being true once edges are directed.

What I am deliberately not telling you

This lesson gave you three things: the inversion, the flipped condition, and boundary seeding. It did not assemble them into either problem you're about to meet.

  • P6 has more than one boundary, and the answer is not "which cells reach this one".
  • P7 is not about flow at all. The grid is undirected there, so GT = G and the inversion buys you nothing — but "start from the boundary" still does. Ask yourself what the boundary is an exception to.

L4 — BFS: Layers Are Distances

Graphs track · lesson 4 of the main line · ~25 min · 2026-08-25 · prereq: L2 — DFS with Cycles, L3 — Copying a Graph

The problem first — "how many days", not "can it reach"

A rumor starts at person 0 in a friendship network. Every day, everyone who knows it tells all their friends. The question is not whether person 3 will hear it — your L2 DFS answers that. The question is: on which day?

Take the square network below: edges 0–1, 1–2, 2–3, 3–0. Run your L2 DFS from 0 and watch the order it reaches 3:

DFS dives: 0 → 1 → 2 → 3. It reaches 3 at depth 3 — but 3 is a direct friend of 0 and hears the rumor on day 1. Recursion depth is the length of the path DFS happened to walk, not the shortest one. DFS answers reachability; it is structurally incapable of answering distance.

The traversal that answers "which day" is BFS — breadth-first search. Same graph, same visited discipline you already own, one swapped part. By the end you'll have the template, the reason layers equal distances, and the multi-source trick — the pieces that P4 (Rotting Oranges, LC 994) will ask you to assemble.

The tool: a line you serve from the front

Three new nouns before any code, all visible in one picture. A queue is a waiting line: things join at the back and are served from the front. That discipline has a name — FIFO, first in, first out: whoever has waited longest is served next. Joining the line is called enqueue; being served (and leaving) is called dequeue.

TermIn the picture

FIFO7 arrived before 9, so 7 leaves before 9 — first in, first out

Contrast with what recursion gave you for free in DFS: the call stack is LIFO — last in, first out — the newest pending thing is handled first, which is exactly why DFS dives. Swap LIFO for FIFO and the traversal stops diving and starts rippling.

The stdlib queue: collections.deque

Python ships the queue. Its contract, before we reason with it (the docs call it a "double-ended queue", pronounced "deck"):

Why not just use a list, appending to the back and popping index 0? The docs answer directly: deques support "appends and pops from either side of the deque with approximately the same O(1) performance", while lists "incur O(n) memory movement costs for pop(0) and insert(0, v)" — popping the front of a list shifts every remaining element one slot left. Do that V times and your O(V + E) traversal quietly becomes O(V²). (No inspect.getsource moment this time: deque is implemented in C, so there is no Python source to open — the docs are the contract.)

After q = deque([7]), then q.append(8), then q.append(9) — what does q.popleft() return, and what remains in q?

append joins at the right (the back of the line), popleft serves from the left (the front). 7 has waited longest, so 7 leaves first — FIFO. Returning 9 would be a stack (LIFO): that's the call-stack discipline DFS runs on.

Mark on enqueue, not on dequeue — mark-on-entry in its third outfit

The unifying rule since L2: mark a vertex the moment it is claimed, not the moment it is processed. In DFS, claiming = entering the call. In BFS, claiming = joining the queue — because between append and popleft a vertex just sits there, and if it isn't marked yet, a second neighbor will discover it and enqueue it again. Trace the triangle 1–2–3 from 1 with the mark moved to the popleft line: pop 1, enqueue 2 and 3 (neither marked). Pop 2, mark it — its neighbor 3 is still unmarked, so 3 is enqueued again. Queue: [3, 3]. On dense graphs the duplicates pile toward one per edge, and the queue you budgeted at V entries swells toward 2E — the same late-mark disease as L2's mark-after-loop and L3's register-after-wiring.

In the BFS template, move seen.add so it marks u right after popleft, instead of marking v at append time. What is the consequence?

Unmarked waiting vertices are invisible to the v not in seen guard, so every extra neighbor that discovers them enqueues another copy. Nothing crashes and nothing is skipped — the traversal still terminates and still reaches everything. It just does redundant work: the queue swells toward 2E instead of staying ≤ V.

Why layers ARE distances

Here is the claim that makes BFS the shortest-path tool: the queue serves vertices in order of their distance from the start. Everything at distance d is served before anything at distance d + 1. The set of vertices at distance d is called layer d (MIT's 6.006 calls them levels); the layer currently being served is the frontier — the rumor's wavefront, the people hearing it today.

Read the popleft column: 0, then 1, 2, then 3, then 4 — layer 0, layer 1, layer 2, layer 3, in order, never interleaved. Why must this always hold? Two facts, both visible in the trace:

  1. Only vertices being served at distance d can discover new ones — and a neighbor of a distance-d vertex is at distance at most d + 1. So everything enters the queue with a distance at most one more than the frontier being served.
  2. FIFO serves in arrival order — so all of layer d (which arrived while serving layer d − 1) is served before anything that arrived after it. The queue never holds more than two consecutive layers at once.

Consequence: the first time BFS reaches a vertex is via a shortest path. That's the theorem the whole shortest-path family stands on — MIT 6.006 Lecture 9 proves it by induction on the levels; Erickson's chapter 5 derives it from "whatever-first search" with a FIFO bag.

BFS from 0 on the opening square (edges 0–1, 1–2, 2–3, 3–0). In which layer is vertex 2 served?

Serving 0 enqueues its direct friends 1 and 3 — that's layer 1. Serving layer 1, both 1 and 3 try to enqueue vertex 2; the first attempt marks and enqueues it, the second bounces off seen. Layer 3 is the DFS route's depth — exactly the wrong answer BFS exists to avoid.

Counting the layers in code — the snapshot loop

The trace shows layers, but the template never says "layer" anywhere. To count them (day numbers, shortest distances), serve one whole layer per turn of an inner loop:

from collections import deque

def dist_from(adj: dict[int, list[int]], start: int) -> dict[int, int]:
    seen: set[int] = {start}
    queue: deque[int] = deque([start])
    dist: dict[int, int] = {}
    d = 0
    while queue:
        for _ in range(len(queue)):     # ← serve exactly ONE layer
            u = queue.popleft()
            dist[u] = d
            for v in adj[u]:
                if v not in seen:
                    seen.add(v)
                    queue.append(v)
        d += 1                          # the queue now holds exactly the next layer
    return dist

The load-bearing line is for _ in range(len(queue)), and it works because of a Python contract worth stating explicitly: range(len(queue)) is evaluated once, when the for starts. If the queue holds 2 vertices, the inner loop runs exactly 2 turns — the appends that happen during those turns grow the queue but not the range. So each turn of the inner loop serves a snapshot: precisely the vertices that were waiting when the layer began, i.e. one full layer. When the inner loop ends, everything it enqueued is the next layer, and d += 1 is the day advancing.

(The common alternative — enqueue (vertex, dist) pairs and skip the inner loop — computes the same distances. The snapshot loop is the roadmap's workhorse because several problems ask "how many rounds", not "what is each vertex's distance".)

Over one whole BFS run on a connected undirected graph: how many times does queue.append execute (counting the seed), and how many v not in seen checks happen?

Appends are capped by seen: one claim per vertex, so V. Checks happen once per neighbor-list entry, and an undirected edge is stored in both endpoints' lists — 2E entries, 2E checks. Same count as Clone Graph's calls-from-the-loop: the 2E is a property of the adjacency structure, not of any particular algorithm.

Multi-source: seed the queue with a crowd

Last mechanism. The rumor question flips: several people know the secret on day 0, and you want, for every person, the day it reaches them — the distance to the nearest source. New algorithm? No. Look at what the seed line actually claims: "these vertices are at distance 0." Nothing in the machine requires that to be one vertex:

queue: deque[int] = deque(sources)   # a crowd at distance 0, not a single start
seen: set[int] = set(sources)        # all of them claimed before day 1 begins

That is the entire change — the while loop, the snapshot, the marking discipline are untouched. Layer d then means "reachable in d steps from some source", and since layers are served in order, the first arrival at each vertex is from its nearest source. Trace it on a line of 7 vertices with sources at 0 and 5:

Day 0 serves {0, 5}; day 1 serves {1, 4, 6}; day 2 serves {2, 3} — the two waves ripple toward each other and meet in the middle, and seen is what stops them from washing over each other. Running one BFS per source and taking minimums would also be correct — and would cost O(sources × (V + E)). Seeding the crowd pays O(V + E) once, total.
